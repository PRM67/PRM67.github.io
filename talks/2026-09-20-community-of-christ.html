My name is Paige Merkley. I was just called two weeks ago to be in the Relief Society presidency for the stake, and I have no idea what I'm doing. But I have felt so much love from the stake presidency, and I hope you know that the stake presidency loves our stake so much, and they have so many goals and so many hopes for our stake. I am very grateful for the opportunity that I have to speak today, because I have been able to learn a lot about the message that I want to share with you all.

Before I begin, though, I wanted to share one of my favorite stories that my dad tells about his mission. He served in Norway, which means that he was in the MTC for eight weeks. Towards the beginning of his time in the MTC, Elder Richard G. Scott came and spoke to the missionaries. It was a great message; they were able to learn a lot, and then they just went on with their daily lives in the MTC.

Towards the end of my dad's MTC stay, they heard that there was going to be a General Authority coming to speak to the missionaries, and he and his district decided to get there early so that they could get good seats. And it was Elder Richard G. Scott that came to speak. It was a great message, but it was pretty much the same message that he had given just a few weeks prior.

Because my dad and the other missionaries were in the front, they got to go shake Elder Scott's hand. And to the shock and amazement of my dad and some of the other missionaries, one of the elders went up to Elder Scott, shook his hand, and said, “Hey, great message. I even liked it a second time.” And Elder Scott was very quick to respond: “Well, good. Maybe something stuck this time.”

Every time I hear that story, I am reminded that every time I hear a message for the second or third time, it means something hasn't stuck yet, and that there's something I still need to learn. So as I share my message today, which is very similar to the messages that have been shared in other wards, I would invite you to listen to the Spirit and try to pick out what still needs to stick for you.

The topic that the stake has given us to share on in different wards is creating community in our wards. But I would add the importance of creating a community of Christ in our wards. Christ is the absolute perfect example of how we treat others, and ultimately it is only through Him that we are able to be brothers and sisters all together.

Something that has been on my mind a lot recently, and I really believe it affects the communities that we are in, is the words that we use to speak about others. In the Bible, there are many accounts of how the Savior is particular with His words. One of the greatest examples is the story of the woman taken in adultery. The men that bring her to the Savior accuse and mock her. They talk about her as if she's an object to be discarded, a walking sin. Though the Savior could have condemned her right there in front of everyone, instead He chose His words very carefully. Rather than chew her out, He invited all of the men around her to reflect on their own sins and mistakes, invoking in them the sense that they cannot blame her if they themselves are sinful as well.

As the prophet of God, the Savior's words teach us that we should never use hateful language. We should never blame and point fingers. After the men leave, the Savior meets her where she is at and calls her “woman.” He stands up for her, and then He invites her to strive to be better. When we are in a situation where someone is being laughed at, talked poorly about, mocked, or made fun of, are we someone that joins in because it is easy? Or are we striving to follow the Savior's example and stand up for them, even if we're the only one?

In our wards, communication is vital. We must be communicating with others if we want them to feel welcomed and important. But please ensure that the words we are using are ones that we would want others to hear. I recently read a talk titled “Your Name Is Safe in Our Home,” and it follows the idea that parents teach their children that everyone's name is safe in their home. As I have thought about this, I know that my name is always safe with the Savior. He will never talk badly about me. What would our wards be like if we could create a community where our standard of communication is the Savior's, where everyone's name is safe within our ward because we want to build others up and not tear them down?

I recently began reading a book called Leaders Eat Last. It is based on the principle that in the military, the leaders are the last ones to eat—the higher-up officials are the last ones to eat at mealtime, and the ones that have just come in are the first ones to eat. It basically talks about how good leaders are willing to serve in the community in which they lead. In this book, there is an analogy that has been on my mind a lot. The author writes:

“The Spartans were a warrior society in ancient Greece. They were feared and revered for their strength, courage and endurance. The power of the Spartan army did not come from the sharpness of their spears, however; it came from the strength of their shields. Losing one's shield in battle was considered the single greatest crime a Spartan could commit. Spartans excused without penalty the warrior who lost his helmet or breastplate in battle, but punished with the loss of all citizenship rights the man who discarded his shield. The reason was simple. A warrior carries his helmet and breastplate for his own protection, but his shield for the safety of the whole line.”

I love this idea of carrying a shield for the protection of the whole line—the attitude of going into battle with the focus not on how others will protect me, but on how I can help and what I can do to protect others. And this should be the same within our wards.

I remember on my mission reading Mark 10:45: “For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister.” As we go throughout our Sunday worship, as we attend different ward functions, it can be easy to ask, “Well, what's in it for me?” I fully believe that we have every right to ponder on how we will be benefited by church services. However, I do not think that this question ever crossed the Savior's mind. Instead, I imagine the Savior always approaching situations asking, “What can I do for others?” Brothers and sisters, as we approach our wards with the attitude to serve in whatever calling we are given, or to serve even if we don't hold a current calling, the community created is one that will bless all of our lives, because we are all holding up our shields.

Elder Uchtdorf, in his talk “Lift Where You Stand,” shared:

“We do not know the names of any of the 2,000 young sons of Helaman. As individuals, they are unnamed. As a group, however, their name will always be remembered for honesty, courage, and the willingness to serve. They accomplished together what none of them could have accomplished alone.”

He goes on to say, “No calling is beneath us. Every calling provides an opportunity to serve and to grow.”

I know that there are opportunities to serve all around us. We are all going through stressful and overwhelming times, but something I read recently has invited me to reflect on how I am doing with this. In the talk “Latter-day Samaritans,” the speaker offers many applicable lessons that we can learn from the Good Samaritan. One that I thought a lot about, though, is when he shared that the Samaritan “went to him.” He did not wait to be approached by the one in need, but rather perceived the need and stepped forth without being asked to do so.

Now, initially, when I hear this, I think about how I don't want to overstep or offend someone by assuming that they need help. I also don't want things to be awkward. But we have been invited to have eyes to see, and we can pray for these eyes. We can pray for our eyes to be opened, to know what we can do to serve in our wards, to serve our neighbors, and to serve our ministering brothers and sisters. It is truly such an amazing blessing, and I can testify of the joy that you feel when the Savior is able to work through your hands.

I know that the Savior loves each and every one of us. I know that He is the perfect example of how we should treat others. I know that none of us are perfect, but that we are all invited to stand together and hold fast to our shields of faith. We are invited to welcome everyone in our wards, to love, to serve, and to see them. And I know that as we do this, we can create a community of Christ. And I say that in the name of Jesus Christ, amen.
